Nobody in this room is getting replaced by a chatbot.

What follows is not a sales pitch. We're going to spend the first half of this on the things AI gets wrong, because you can't use a tool you don't trust, and you shouldn't trust one you've only heard promises about.

03 · where it breaks

The failure modes, before the features

These are not bugs waiting for a patch. They're properties of how the thing works. Learn them once and you stop being surprised.

  • 01It is confidently wrongEspecially about version numbers, prices, dates, and anything it half-remembers. It never sounds unsure. Check anything you'd be embarrassed to be wrong about.
  • 02It forgets everything between chatsEach conversation starts from zero. Nothing carries over unless you paste it in again. This is a feature for privacy and a chore for you.
  • 03It agrees with you too easilySay a bad plan with confidence and it will help you execute the bad plan. Ask "what's wrong with this?" — you have to invite the pushback.
  • 04Long chats rotOnce a wrong assumption is in the conversation, it sticks and spreads. When answers start drifting, don't argue — open a new chat.
  • 05It approximates instead of countingArithmetic, tallies, "how many rows" — treat with suspicion unless it shows the work or runs code.
The rule that covers all five: you don't send anything you couldn't defend in a meeting. The output is a draft. You're still the one signing it.
04 · what it's genuinely good at

Now the useful part

Notice the pattern: every one of these is a job where a fast rough draft is more valuable than a slow perfect one, because you're going to edit it anyway.

  • ✓The blank page problemEmails, summaries, meeting notes, job descriptions, first outlines. Getting to a bad draft in 30 seconds is worth more than staring at nothing for 30 minutes.
  • ✓Explaining unfamiliar things at your levelPaste a document, a contract clause, an error message, a spreadsheet formula. Ask for it in plain language. Then ask a follow-up. This is the single most underused thing it does.
  • ✓Turning mess into structureTwelve scattered notes into a table. A rambling thread into three decisions and an owner for each.
  • ✓Being the person you think out loud atIt has infinite patience for your half-formed idea at 6pm. Ask it to argue the other side.
  • ✓Boring translation workBetween languages, formats, tones, or "make this the same thing but for a customer instead of my boss."
05 · the one skill

The whole skill is telling it what you actually want

Same request, same model, thirty seconds apart. The difference is not magic words — it's the context you already have in your head and didn't say.

vague→ generic
Write an email about the delay.
specific
Write an email to a client telling them our delivery slips by one month. Cause: supplier shortage, not our error. Tone: apologetic but not groveling. They are already annoyed — this is the second delay. Must include: new date, what we're doing about it, no excuses paragraph. Under 150 words.
Four questions turn any vague ask into a good one: who is it for · what's the situation · what must it include · how long. That's it. That's the course.
06 · today, not someday

Your first twenty minutes

Don't start with your hardest problem. Start with something boring you already know the right answer to — that's how you calibrate how much to trust it.

  • 1Pick the dullest thing you did yesterdayAsk for a first draft using the four questions. Judge the draft like you'd judge a junior colleague's. Six minutes.
  • 2Paste something you didn't fully understandA document, a log, a policy. Ask for plain language. Then ask: "what part of this are you least sure about?" Six minutes.
  • 3Write down the one prompt that workedSave it. Send it to one colleague. A team gets good at this by sharing prompts, not by attending talks. Eight minutes.
If nothing works today, that's real information and worth saying out loud. "I tried it on X and it was useless" is a more useful contribution than enthusiasm.
